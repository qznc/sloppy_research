title: Compute on Credit: AI's Trillion-Dollar Race Against Rising Rates
date: 2026-10-21
tldr: AI industry not only needs to grow a lot to be profitable, it also needs to grow fast enough.

S&P Global Ratings (27 August 2026) projects that AI infrastructure investment will exceed $1.3 trillion by 2027.
That scale raises a question capability benchmarks cannot answer: can the economy generate enough durable revenue and productivity gains to justify the capital being committed?

AI can be a genuine technological breakthrough and still attract excessive investment.
The tension lies in timing.
Capability can improve far faster than companies, institutions, and workers can redesign their processes around it.
A technology that ultimately transforms the economy can still disappoint financially in the near term if investors price in returns before adaptation occurs.

## The scale of the build-out

FactSet reports that hyperscalers are increasingly turning to external financing as CapEx outpaces internally generated cash—a shift from build-outs funded primarily from operating cash flow toward expansion that also depends on debt (FactSet, 2026).
Reuters reported on 19 October 2026 that S&P Global warned leading hyperscalers likely carry more AI-linked debt than is currently known.

The investment total alone does not prove the boom is unsustainable.
Data centres and computing equipment can support many products and revenue streams over their useful lives.
But the scale establishes the challenge: the infrastructure must be utilised, priced, and monetised well enough to cover operating costs, financing costs, depreciation, and investor returns.

## The revenue hurdle

Goldman Sachs (September 2026) estimates that six major hyperscalers need $1.42 trillion in AI revenue by 2028–2030 to justify their compute CapEx.
*The Economist* (28 July 2026) models a larger hurdle: with big-tech CapEx heading toward $900 billion in 2026 and $1.4 trillion in 2027, the industry requires approximately $2.5 trillion per year in AI-driven revenue to cover depreciation and earn a modest return on invested capital.

Those estimates are not directly comparable.
A requirement for multiple trillions annually by 2027–2028 is a different figure from a $1.42 trillion target over the 2028–2030 period.
Both depend on assumptions about equipment lifetimes, utilisation, energy and operating costs, margins, and which revenues fairly count as "AI."

Digital Applied's compilation of Gartner, IDC, and Stanford forecasts puts current annual AI spending at around $150–200 billion.
Against the Economist's $2.5 trillion hurdle, the implied growth factor is roughly 10–15×.
"Spending" and "required revenue" count different things—one includes infrastructure expenditure, the other counts provider revenue—so the comparison signals the order of magnitude of the challenge, not a precise arithmetic requirement.

The central question is whether revenue, margins, and measurable productivity gains can scale quickly enough to cover the investments already made and those still planned.
A shortfall would not prove AI lacks long-term value; it would mean the timing or distribution of that value did not match the financial expectations embedded in current investment plans and valuations.

## Is the gap 10× or larger?

Even the scale of today's AI market is hard to assess.
Some spending reflects customers paying for demonstrable business outcomes; some is funded by venture capital that lets unproven companies buy AI services before their business model is sustainable.
That expenditure is real revenue for a provider, but not evidence of durable end-user demand.

If a significant share of spending depends on speculative financing, the underlying demand base is smaller than headline totals suggest, and the growth multiple required to reach a self-sustaining level of revenue would be higher.
Multiples of 20× or 30× are possible scenarios, not established forecasts; they depend on how much current activity proves self-sustaining.

The spend that does exist is heavily concentrated.
Ramp reports that 1% of firms account for roughly 80% of OpenAI and Anthropic spending, with that top cohort averaging $9,568 per employee per month in September 2026—3.1% below its July high of $9,873—while the median Ramp customer spends an order of magnitude less (Kharazian, 14 October 2026).
Whether that concentration reflects genuine enterprise value or a small set of well-funded heavy users is hard to separate.
Ramp's adoption measure reached 55.9% of its businesses paying for at least one AI model in September, but growth in new AI customers has slowed to gains of roughly half a point a month; Kharazian argues the market is approaching the top of the adoption S-curve, where growth must come from more intense use rather than new customers.

TechCrunch (9 September 2026) reported that AI spending per employee fell at several prominent firms in August 2026.
A single month can reflect seasonality, pricing, or budget timing as much as demand, so the reading is not conclusive.
The stronger conclusion is narrower: the industry needs to show that adoption translates into recurring, economically justified spending—not merely that users experiment with increasingly capable systems.

## Token growth is not spending growth

Ramp's AI Index (Ara Kharazian, Ramp Economics Lab) documents a divergence since around summer 2026: token consumption continued to grow while corporate dollar spending plateaued.
The September reading reported blended token prices roughly 41% lower, as companies shifted workloads from closed frontier models to cheaper alternatives (Kharazian, 9 September 2026).
The October update recorded two consecutive months of virtually flat token spend—August and September—with Ramp attributing the slowdown to frontier price declines and efficiency gains rather than seasonality (Kharazian, 14 October 2026).
At the start of October, AI spend rose to a new peak about 1.5% above the previous August high, so the series is stalling rather than collapsing.

Tokens are a useful proxy for model use, but what users pay is also affected by falling prices, more efficient models, and changes in task mix.
That distinction cuts both ways.
A spending plateau could indicate reluctance to expand budgets; it could also mean customers are getting more computation for the same money—positive if lower costs unlock broader adoption.
Ramp's customer base, which skews toward startups and heavy AI users, is informative but not representative of the whole economy.

On contracts, Ramp reports that enterprise deals at OpenAI and Anthropic have so far been flexible rather than restrictive: customers are typically not committing to higher-than-normal spend, and the labs are offering discounts from sticker prices when they do (Kharazian, 14 October 2026).
That favours buyers today but means pricing pressure can feed lower dollar revenue without necessarily driving additional volume.

TechCrunch's per-employee spending data adds a more enterprise-focused signal of caution.
Together, the data support closer scrutiny, not a claim that demand has entered structural decline.

## Rising rates raise the bar

US real yields reached 2.91% as of 7 October 2026 (MacroRadar), and the Federal Reserve's 10-year TIPS reopening in September 2026 priced at a real yield of 2.653%, the highest in nearly 18 years (Tipswatch, 17 September 2026).

Reuters (15 October 2026) reported that benchmark 10-year US Treasury yields posted their sharpest quarterly rise this century, and that the resulting selloff is forcing companies driving the AI build-out to delay fundraising or pay more for capital.
In the same week, listings for the Blackstone-backed AirTrunk REIT, Nvidia-backed Firmus, and SoftBank-backed SB Energy were pulled or paused, and investors began demanding proof that the sector can absorb higher interest rates and computing-power costs.

Higher yields raise the cost of borrowing and the return investors require from long-duration projects.
Data centres and specialised computing infrastructure have large upfront costs and payback horizons that depend on future utilisation and cash flows.
When the discount rate rises, projects that looked attractive under cheaper financing can become marginal.

Fiscal and geopolitical strains add to the uncertainty.
Moody's stripped the US of its last AAA rating in 2026 (LBBW, 2026), while export controls on rare-earth elements and oil-price pressure add to supply-chain and inflation risk (MERICS, 2026; ZDFheute, 2026).
These are not direct evidence about AI demand, but they affect the financing environment.

Higher capital costs do not make the build-out uneconomic by definition, but they leave less room for delays, underutilisation, and weaker-than-expected pricing.

## Equity investors and bondholders are asking different questions

The shift toward external financing changes who judges the AI build-out.
Equity investors can tolerate uncertain timelines if they believe a company may eventually dominate a large market.
Venture capital is built around this logic.
Credit investors have a different priority: will a borrower generate enough cash to pay interest and principal on schedule, even if the most optimistic growth scenario does not materialise?

This difference is increasingly relevant as CapEx absorbs cash that companies might otherwise use for buybacks.
Axios (16 June 2026) reported that AI investments are consuming cash flows and constraining buybacks.
J.P. Morgan Asset Management (2026) has raised the question of whether credit markets can absorb the build-out at its current scale.

The scrutiny is also changing the mix of buyers.
Reuters (15 October 2026) reports that the conversation among investors has shifted "from growth at any cost to a much sharper focus on quality," and that investors are now differentiating between established wholesale operators with long-term customer contracts and riskier "neoclouds" and AI-campus builders.
That is a selective repricing, not a sector-wide retreat.

Debt does not automatically make an investment reckless.
It does impose a timetable.
If utilisation or revenue arrives later than expected, a company may need to refinance, raise additional capital, delay projects, or accept lower returns.
Credit-market pricing is therefore an early-warning indicator, read alongside rates, liquidity, and the structure of each financing arrangement.

## Two different bets: infrastructure, model labs, and open weights

The AI trade contains at least two distinct bets.
The first is that hyperscalers and other infrastructure owners will earn adequate returns on data centres, GPUs, power, and networking.
The second is that frontier AI labs will capture enough revenue and margin from their models to justify their own valuations and operating costs.

Those outcomes can diverge.
If open-weight models compress prices charged by closed-model providers, AI labs could see margins squeezed even as overall token use rises.
Infrastructure owners could still benefit from additional compute demand, provided competition and capacity expansion do not push their own prices down too far.
The reverse is also possible: a successful model provider could build a valuable business without generating enough incremental demand to make every planned data centre profitable.

Open models are the main channel for that margin pressure.
NVIDIA's push toward frontier-level open models—alongside its existing Nemotron offering—illustrates the shift: a dominant chip supplier has reason to support a broad ecosystem of models that keeps its hardware in demand, independent of any single closed AI lab.
Chinese developers and other open-weight providers are expanding the range of capable alternatives (SemiAnalysis, 2026; Layer3 Labs, 2026).
As model quality converges, buyers gain bargaining power and shift workloads between providers more easily.

That is positive for adoption: cheaper models make it easier to deploy AI in more products and workflows.
The difficulty falls on AI labs that rely on premium pricing to cover training and inference costs.
More usage does not necessarily mean more profit for every participant in the value chain.
Investors should assess the economics of compute infrastructure separately from the economics of model development and distribution.

## Oracle and the warning from credit markets

Oracle has become a prominent test case for the financial risks surrounding AI infrastructure.
The company has taken on substantial commitments related to data-centre expansion under Project Jupiter, which has encountered operational, financing, and permitting challenges in New Mexico (Source NM, 24 August 2026; Albuquerque Journal, 2026).

FinanceFeeds (2026) reported that Project Jupiter loans were trading at about 90 cents on the dollar and Oracle bonds at about 85 cents.
Bond prices at those levels signal that investors demand compensation for heightened risk, though they do not by themselves establish imminent default.
Yahoo Finance (2026) reported that Oracle triggered force-majeure clauses on a data-centre project.
Whether that signals isolated execution problems or broader stress depends on facts not yet in public filings.

The pattern is not confined to Oracle.
Reuters (15 October 2026) reported that the debt of data-centre operator CoreWeave has slumped toward distressed levels; Firmus, which had aimed to raise $5 billion in what would have been Australia's second-largest listing, struggled to convince investors on its revenue projections while carrying about $30 billion of debt; and Holtec pulled its US listing citing uncertainty about data-centre demand.
At the same time, capital is being reallocated rather than withdrawn: Dealogic counts $21.3 billion of M&A involving Asian data centres this year, more than double all of 2025, and $22.3 billion of syndicated loans and bond issuance, while equity fundraising is lower (Reuters, 15 October 2026).

Private-credit markets are under scrutiny too.
Blue Owl's chief executives pushed back against sector fears in October 2026 (Semafor, 8 October 2026), while reports describe banks offering hedge funds ways to short private-credit exposure (Bloomberg Law; Hedgeweek, 2026)—debate and hedging, not proof of systemic crisis.
The risk to watch is whether losses or refinancing stress spread from isolated projects into a wider pullback in credit availability.

## The capability gains are real; adoption is the bottleneck

Financial caution should not be confused with technological scepticism.
A concrete case: a GPT-5-generated hypothesis—a novel application of the Tomonaga-Schwinger formulation to nonlinear quantum mechanics—passed peer review and was published in *Physics Letters B* (arXiv:2511.15935, December 2025).
AI can be powerful and uneven across tasks at the same time.

The bottleneck is increasingly organisational.
To turn model capability into financial results, companies must redesign workflows, establish oversight, train employees, integrate systems with existing software and data, and decide which tasks to automate or augment.
Universities, government agencies, and other institutions face similar frictions.

McKinsey's 2026 survey of AI adoption describes a widening gap between adoption and the ability to convert productivity improvements into profit.
Buying access to a model is relatively easy; changing how an organisation operates is harder.
Those gains surface as better service, faster decisions, or new products, but not immediately as measurable cost savings or revenue.

That lag is central to the financial argument.
Models can improve on a compressed technical timeline, while organisational change takes years.
If investors price in rapid monetisation, delayed adoption hurts returns without invalidating the technology itself.

## Is this another dot-com bubble?

Comparisons with the late-1990s technology boom are instructive but imperfect.
As then, investors are funding a major technological shift while expectations for future growth drive current valuations.
Some projects may be overbuilt, and some companies may not survive.

Peter Wildeford (29 October 2025) makes the opposing case: AI companies are unlike the dot-com failures—they have product-market fit, real revenue, and reasonable unit economics once infrastructure outlay is set aside.
If the build-out fails, he argues, it would be an infrastructure bubble of the type seen in the 1840s railway mania or the 1997–2002 telecom build-out, where the assets eventually find use but too late for those who paid.
He also notes a relevant difference from 1999: today's boom funds real computing infrastructure with substantial use, data centres are utilised immediately, and GPUs are more flexible than specialised fibre.

That framing does not settle the question, but it changes its character.
The risk is less that AI is worthless and more that too much capacity is priced as if demand arrives sooner than it will.
Real technology and real demand do not guarantee that every investment is attractively priced; the question is where expected returns exceed reasonable assumptions.

Financial outcomes are likely to vary by company.
Forbes (21 May 2026) describes contrasting paths to profitability for Anthropic and OpenAI.
ValueAddVC analysis puts Anthropic's Q2 2026 operating profit at $559 million—a milestone—while OpenAI's position is weaker than headline losses suggest.
FutureSearch (Dan Schwarz, updated 16 October 2026) forecasts a 2026 GAAP loss near $63 billion against the widely cited $14 billion non-GAAP figure, and profitability arriving in 2031 or later.
Its IPO is the point where these assumptions meet public-market discipline.

## What forecasters expect

Forecasting platforms provide a second, opinion-weighted cross-check beyond credit pricing.
On Metaculus, the community's estimate for Anthropic's IPO date was 3 December 2026, with a central range of roughly 17 November 2026 to 18 January 2027 (19 forecasters, snapshot 27 September 2026).
On the same platform, the question of which lab would carry the higher valuation on 1 January 2027 stood at about 25% OpenAI against 75% Anthropic (21 forecasters, snapshot 24 August 2026).

These are not settled forecasts; they move with daily news.
The direction is nonetheless consistent with the financial divergence described above: late-2026 collective judgment favoured Anthropic on both the timing of a listing and the near-term valuation.
The market that prices the build-out continuously is the credit market—bonds and default swaps on Oracle, CoreWeave, and the hyperscalers—because it prices repayment obligations, not narrative.

## The bull case—and what would change the verdict

A recent spending plateau may be temporary, with costs falling enough to make wider deployment economical.
Some AI labs may defend margins through better products, enterprise relationships, or differentiated capabilities.

The steelman for the bulls points to revenue that already exists.
OpenAI's annualised revenue grew from about $0.2 billion in March 2023 to roughly $13 billion by August 2025 (Wildeford, 29 October 2025).
Wildeford argues the binding constraint on AI today is supply—power, land, and compute—rather than demand, that data centres are being filled as soon as they open, and that the extreme scenario (AI that automates large parts of the economy) would make today's required-revenue hurdles look small.
That scenario also illustrates the tail risk: if capabilities arrive on schedule, the financiers bet against the bears; if they do not, the bears bet against the financiers.

That case is now a year old, so it can be checked against what happened since.
The revenue-growth claim has held: OpenAI's run rate reached roughly $50 billion by late September 2026, several times the $13 billion Wildeford cited (FutureSearch, 16 October 2026).
The confidence that the financing structure was fundamentally sound, and that supply rather than demand was the binding constraint, has aged worse: the most leveraged builders have come under the credit pressure described above (Oracle's bonds trade at about 85 cents on the dollar), and Ramp's data show token spending flat through August and September before a marginal recovery in early October (Kharazian, 14 October 2026).
The distinction Wildeford drew, real revenue and utilisation versus speculative unit economics, has if anything sharpened; the dispute is now about timing and margins, not whether AI has revenue.

Several indicators should help resolve the debate:

- **Revenue quality**: Are AI revenues recurring and supported by customers' own cash flows, or dependent heavily on investment-funded spending?
- **Unit economics**: Are inference costs falling faster than prices, and are providers retaining margins as usage scales?
- **Enterprise returns**: Can customers demonstrate measurable gains after implementation, integration, and oversight costs?
- **Infrastructure utilisation**: Are new data centres and GPUs used at levels that justify their financing and depreciation costs?
- **Credit conditions**: Are spreads, refinancing terms, and project-level debt pricing deteriorating beyond what broader rates and liquidity explain?
- **Competitive dynamics**: As open models improve, does additional usage expand the overall market enough to offset price and margin pressure?

A sustained recovery in paid usage, demonstrable enterprise returns, high infrastructure utilisation, and resilient margins would strengthen the bull case.
Persistent spending weakness, falling prices without compensating volume growth, delayed projects, or deteriorating credit terms would strengthen the sceptical one.

## Conclusion

The AI debate is not a choice between technological revolution and financial bubble.
Both can coexist.
Capabilities can advance rapidly while the companies financing the infrastructure earn disappointing returns.
A technology can transform the economy over time and still be overbuilt, overvalued, or financed on terms that assume adoption happens too quickly.

For infrastructure investors, the question is whether durable compute demand will produce sufficient cash flow regardless of which models win.
For AI labs, the question is whether they can capture enough margin from that activity to cover their costs and justify their valuations.
For enterprise customers, the test is whether AI changes productivity and business outcomes—not merely usage statistics.

The $1.3 trillion infrastructure projection and the growing reliance on external financing underline the scale of the bet.
Higher rates make the margin for error smaller, and credit markets have begun to distinguish more sharply between solid projects and speculative ones (Reuters, 15 October 2026).
The ultimate outcome will depend less on whether models can do impressive things than on how quickly businesses reorganise around them, how much customers pay, and which companies capture the resulting value.

The most plausible risk is not that AI proves useless.
It is that the technology succeeds on a longer timetable than capital markets currently expect.
The probability that at least one major hyperscaler faces a meaningful refinancing stress event before 2029 is roughly 40–50%; the probability that AI-specific revenue reaches the $1.42 trillion threshold by 2030 is below 30%—though either estimate could shift substantially on six months of spending data.
(Note: "AI spending", "AI revenue", and "AI infrastructure investment" are defined differently across the sources cited; the revenue hurdles are scenario-dependent estimates, not settled industry-wide requirements.)

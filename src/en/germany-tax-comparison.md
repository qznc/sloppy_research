title: How Germany's tax and social contributions compare internationally
date: 2026-10-08
tldr: Germany is not an extreme outlier in the overall amount of taxation it raises, but it is an extreme case in the way a large share of that burden falls on labour.

Germany is both a high-tax country and a country with an unusually high tax burden on labour. The two statements appear contradictory but are not.

Germany's tax-to-GDP ratio was 38.0% in 2024, below Denmark, France and Belgium. Yet social-security contributions reached 17.5% of GDP, the highest in the EU. For workers, the tax wedge for a single average worker is 49.3% in 2025, second only to Belgium in the OECD.

The explanation lies in how Germany finances its welfare state. Germany relies on earmarked social-insurance contributions, which appear separately from other taxes and are visible on payslips. Other countries finance similar programmes through general taxation or mandatory private insurance.

This distinction matters. Germany's contribution burden ranks at the top, but its total tax burden is much less exceptional than the payroll deductions suggest. The key question is therefore not simply whether Germans pay high taxes, but which measure is being used and how comparable the underlying systems are.

This article examines those measures and asks where Germany genuinely stands out, where it resembles other welfare states, and where its apparent exceptionalism is mainly a consequence of financing structure.

## Germany's overall tax burden

Germany's tax-to-GDP ratio was 38.1% in 2023 and 38.0% in 2024 (Bundesfinanzministerium, *Monatsbericht Juli 2026*, 2026). This placed it above the OECD average of 34.1% in 2024, but below France at 43.5%, Denmark at 45.2% and Belgium at 45.1% (OECD, *Revenue Statistics 2025*, 2025).

Eurostat's 2024 figures show similarly high ratios in Denmark at 45.8%, France at 45.3%, Belgium at 45.1% and Austria at 43.8% (Eurostat, *Tax revenue statistics*, 2025). Germany's level is high by international standards, but it does not place Germany at the top of the European ranking.

Germany relies heavily on dedicated social-insurance contributions. In 2024, social contributions amounted to 17.5% of GDP, the highest proportion in the EU, compared with 0.7% in Denmark and 3.7% in Sweden (Eurostat, *Tax revenue statistics – Statistics Explained*, 2025).

This helps explain why Germany can simultaneously have a moderate aggregate tax-to-GDP ratio and very high payroll deductions. A large part of the financing of social protection appears as a separate contribution rather than being incorporated into general taxation.

## What the different measures mean

International comparisons often confuse several concepts described as a tax burden.

The **tax-to-GDP ratio** measures total government tax revenue, including social contributions, relative to GDP. It is the best measure for comparing the overall level of taxation collected by government.

**Social contributions as a share of GDP** measure the portion of revenue raised through social-insurance contributions. They tell us more about financing structure than about the total tax burden.

The **tax wedge on labour** measures taxes and social contributions associated with employment relative to total labour costs. It indicates how heavily employment itself is taxed.

**Social expenditure** measures how much is spent on social protection. It is different from tax revenue: a country can finance similar levels of social spending through different combinations of taxation, contributions and private payments.

These distinctions are essential to interpreting Germany's position.

## The German social-contribution model

Germany's social-insurance system finances current costs primarily through contributions from employees and employers. The system covers pensions, health insurance, long-term care and unemployment insurance, while occupational accident insurance is financed by employers (Germany Trade & Invest, *Employment and Social Insurance in Germany*, 2024/2026).

This is characteristic of the Bismarckian model of social protection, which relies on earmarked payroll contributions rather than general taxation.

Germany's main social-insurance branches include:

**Pension insurance:** the statutory contribution rate is 18.6% of gross wages, divided equally between employers and employees at 9.3% each. The annual contribution ceiling is €96,600 for 2026 (PwC, *Worldwide Tax Summaries*, 2026).

**Health insurance:** the total contribution rate is approximately 16.1%, including the average additional contribution charged by health insurers. Contributions are shared between employers and employees, subject to an annual assessment ceiling of €66,150 in 2026 (PwC, *Worldwide Tax Summaries*, 2026).

**Long-term care insurance:** the standard contribution rate is 3.4%, with an additional contribution for childless individuals over 23 (GTAI, *Employment and Social Insurance in Germany*, 2026).

**Unemployment insurance:** the contribution rate is 2.6%, shared equally between employers and employees (Payroll Service Germany, 2026).

**Accident insurance:** financed by employers alone, with an average contribution rate of approximately 1.09% of payroll, although the actual rate varies by industry and risk classification (GTAI, 2026).

Taken together, these contributions represent a large share of labour income. For employees, the direct social-insurance deductions are roughly 21–22% of wages before income tax, while the combined employer and employee contributions can amount to roughly 43–44% of wages within the relevant contribution ceilings.

At the macro level, these contributions appear as one component of Germany's overall 38.0% tax-to-GDP ratio. They do not represent an additional 17.5% on top of that figure.

That distinction is central to the international comparison.

## Three ways of financing welfare states

Countries can provide similar forms of social protection while financing them in very different ways.

### Bismarckian financing

Germany, France and Austria rely heavily on social-insurance contributions from employers and employees. As a result, social contributions appear as a large separate category in national accounts.

Germany's social contributions amount to 17.5% of GDP, compared with 16.5% in France and 16.0% in Austria (Eurostat, *Tax revenue statistics – Statistics Explained*, 2025).

### Tax-funded financing

Nordic countries rely much more heavily on general taxation to finance social protection.

Sweden's social contributions amount to only 3.7% of GDP, while Denmark's amount to 0.7%. Yet their overall tax-to-GDP ratios are much higher: approximately 42.5% in Sweden and 45.8% in Denmark (Eurostat, *Tax revenue statistics*, 2025).

The low contribution figures in these countries do not imply low taxation or low social spending. A larger share of social protection is financed through general taxation.

### Systems with substantial private mandatory financing

Countries such as the Netherlands and Switzerland make greater use of mandatory private insurance alongside public programmes, while the United States combines public social insurance with a larger private component.

In the Netherlands, public social spending is approximately 22% of GDP, while mandatory private social spending adds roughly 11–13%, producing a broader financing picture (OECD, *Private social expenditure and the influence of tax systems*, 2015; OECD Statistics Blog, 2023).

These systems illustrate an important methodological point: comparing public tax revenue alone can understate the resources devoted to social protection where mandatory private arrangements play a larger role.

## Germany's labour tax burden ranks among the highest

Germany is not an outlier in overall tax-to-GDP terms, but its tax burden on employment is high.

According to OECD *Taxing Wages 2026*, Germany's tax wedge for a single average worker increased to 49.3% in 2025, 1.34 percentage points higher than the previous year. That was the second-highest level in the OECD, behind Belgium, compared with an OECD average of 35.1% (OECD, *Taxing Wages 2026*, 2026).

Germany also has the highest low-wage tax wedge in the EU, at 44.7% (Eurostat, *Wages and labour costs*, 2026).

This creates a macro-micro tension.

At the macro level, Germany's tax-to-GDP ratio is high but not exceptional.

At the level of an individual employee, the combination of income tax and social-insurance contributions creates one of the largest tax wedges in the OECD.

The difference is not a statistical contradiction. The two measures answer different questions.

## Why the burden is concentrated on labour

One reason for Germany's high labour tax wedge is the structure of social insurance itself.

Contribution rates are broadly proportional to earnings up to defined contribution ceilings. Average earners fall below those ceilings, meaning that they pay the full applicable contribution rate on their earnings. Higher earners eventually stop paying additional contributions once the relevant ceiling is reached.

This means that social insurance is not fully progressive in the same way as a progressive income-tax system.

The system also creates an important distinction between what employees see and what economists consider the full labour burden.

Employees see their own social-insurance deductions directly on payslips. Employer contributions are less visible, but they are nevertheless part of the cost of employing labour. Over time, part of those employer costs can be reflected in lower wages rather than remaining with employers (OECD, *Taxing Wages methodology*, 2026).

This is why the economic incidence of payroll contributions cannot be inferred simply from the amount shown as an employee deduction.

## Pension financing

Pensions are one of the largest components of Germany's social expenditure.

Across OECD countries, old-age and survivor pensions account on average for approximately 7.7% of GDP, while health spending represents around 5.8% of GDP (OECD, *Society at a Glance 2024*, 2024).

Germany's public pension expenditure is approximately 11–12% of GDP. That is above the OECD average, but below Italy's level of more than 15% and comparable with France at around 13–14% (OECD, *Society at a Glance 2024*, 2024).

Germany's statutory pension contribution rate of 18.6% is high, but the contribution rate itself should not be interpreted as a direct measure of how much the country ultimately spends on pensions.

The pay-as-you-go structure, demographic composition and financing arrangements all affect expenditure.

The OECD's *Pensions at a Glance 2025* highlights an efficiency question: Germany devotes substantial resources to its pension system while future replacement rates remain below the OECD average (OECD, *Pensions at a Glance 2025*, 2025).

## Health-system financing

Germany also spends a relatively large share of GDP on health: approximately 12–13% of GDP. France is in a similar range, while Sweden spends approximately 9–10% through a more tax-funded system. The United States is substantially higher, at around 16–17% of GDP (OECD, *Society at a Glance 2024*, 2024).

The comparison again shows why financing mechanism and total resource use should not be confused.

Germany's system finances health care primarily through social insurance, whereas Sweden relies more heavily on taxation. A lower social-contribution figure in Sweden does not mean that the country devotes little public money to health care.

## Unemployment insurance

Germany's unemployment insurance contribution rate is 2.6%, shared between employers and employees. Unemployment benefits generally replace around 60–67% of previous earnings and are typically available for up to 12 months, subject to eligibility and individual circumstances (GTAI, *Employment and Social Insurance in Germany*, 2024; OECD, *Society at a Glance 2024*, 2024).

Germany's system is therefore less generous than some European alternatives.

France has higher contribution rates and longer potential benefit durations, while Nordic countries such as Sweden and Denmark have historically provided higher replacement rates through systems that rely more heavily on taxation (OECD, *Society at a Glance 2024*, 2024).

The United States represents the other end of the spectrum, with a lower payroll contribution burden for unemployment-related insurance but generally less generous benefits and shorter maximum durations.

## Long-term care

Germany's long-term care insurance is another distinctive part of its welfare-state model.

Introduced in 1995, the system is financed through a dedicated contribution currently set at 3.4% of wages, with an additional charge for some childless workers. Long-term care expenditure amounts to approximately 1.5–1.8% of GDP (GTAI, *Employment and Social Insurance in Germany*, 2024).

Comparable spending is somewhat higher in Sweden and Denmark, where long-term care is primarily tax-funded, and similar in France, where financing also relies significantly on social contributions.

## What an international comparison can and cannot show

The simplest and most robust international comparison is the tax-to-GDP ratio because the OECD and Eurostat definitions already incorporate social contributions into total tax revenue.

On this basis, Germany's 38.0% tax-to-GDP ratio in 2024 was above the OECD average but below the levels recorded in countries such as France, Denmark and Belgium (OECD, *Revenue Statistics 2025*, 2025; Bundesfinanzministerium, *Monatsbericht Juli 2026*, 2026).

More complicated are comparisons that attempt to combine public taxation with mandatory private social spending. Such comparisons can provide useful context, but they require careful treatment of definitions, timing, tax expenditures and the distinction between public spending and privately financed mandatory arrangements.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that all welfare states have exactly the same social burden once the numbers are adjusted. Rather, the evidence shows that countries can devote substantial resources to social protection while placing those resources in very different statistical and financing categories.

## Germany compared with its peers

Germany stands out in some respects.

Its social contributions of 17.5% of GDP are the highest in the EU. Its employees face substantial direct payroll deductions, and its tax wedge for an average worker is among the highest in the OECD.

Germany is also distinctive in operating a comprehensive long-term-care insurance system funded through dedicated contributions.

At the same time, Germany is not an outlier in overall taxation. Its 38.0% tax-to-GDP ratio is below that of several European welfare states, including France, Denmark, Belgium and Austria.

Its health spending is relatively high, but within the range observed in other advanced economies with comprehensive systems.

Its public pension spending is above the OECD average, but comparable with other large continental European welfare states.

Its unemployment insurance system is less generous than some Nordic and French arrangements.

The resulting picture is mixed rather than one-dimensional.

## The case for calling Germany a high-tax country

There is a strong case for describing Germany as a high-tax country from the perspective of workers and businesses.

The evidence is the tax wedge.

For a single average worker, a 49.3% wedge means that nearly half of the total cost of employing that worker is absorbed by income tax and employee and employer social-security contributions, rather than becoming the worker's net income.

From a business perspective, these non-wage labour costs increase the cost of employment and can affect international competitiveness, particularly in sectors exposed to global competition.

From an employee perspective, the most visible part of the burden is the substantial deduction from gross wages.

From a public-finance perspective, social contributions are economically comparable to labour taxes even though they are accounted for separately from personal income tax.

All three perspectives point toward a high burden on labour.

The evidence does not support the claim that Germany's high-tax reputation is simply an illusion created by accounting conventions. Accounting differences explain why Germany can appear exceptionally burdened in some comparisons and merely high or middle-ranking in others.

## Conclusion: Germany's tax burden is concentrated on labour

Germany's international tax position is best understood through two separate observations.

First, Germany's overall tax-to-GDP ratio is high but not exceptional. At 38.0% of GDP in 2024, it was below the levels recorded by several other major European welfare states.

Second, Germany places an exceptionally large fiscal burden on employment. Social-insurance contributions account for 17.5% of GDP, and the tax wedge for a single average worker reached 49.3% in 2025, second-highest in the OECD.

These findings are not contradictory.

Germany's Bismarckian welfare state finances a large share of social protection through payroll contributions. Denmark and Sweden finance more through general taxation, while countries such as the Netherlands and Switzerland make greater use of mandatory private insurance. The different financing structures change where the burden appears in national accounts and on household budgets.

The most accurate description of Germany is not simply that it is high-tax or not high-tax. Germany is a country with a relatively high overall tax burden and an exceptionally high tax burden on labour. Its distinctive feature is the extent to which social protection is financed through visible, earmarked social-insurance contributions.

That distinction matters for economic policy and public debate. Arguments about Germany's tax burden should specify whether they concern total government revenue, the financing of social protection, the tax wedge on employment, or the amount of social spending produced by those revenues.

Once those measures are separated, the apparent contradiction largely disappears: Germany is not an extreme outlier in the overall amount of taxation it raises, but it is an extreme case in the way a large share of that burden falls on labour.

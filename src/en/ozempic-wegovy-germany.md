title: Ozempic and Wegovy in Germany: how well and how safely do they work in the long run?
date: 2026-10-23
tldr: For people without a medical need, the risk–benefit balance is unfavourable, and in Germany they also bear the full cost.

Two copies of the same molecule are sold in Germany. Ozempic (semaglutide up to 1.0 mg weekly) is licensed for type 2 diabetes and has been reimbursed by the statutory funds for that use. Wegovy is the same active ingredient at up to 2.4 mg weekly, licensed for weight management, and since mid-2023 available in German pharmacies — but only against private payment (NDR, July 2026). The question this article answers: does it keep working long term, and is it safe?

## One molecule, two medicines

Semaglutide mimics the gut hormone GLP-1. It increases fullness, reduces hunger and craving, and slows stomach emptying (tagesschau, July 2026). Wegovy is authorised for adults with a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with a weight-related condition such as diabetes, high blood pressure or sleep apnoea, and for obese adolescents from 12 years (EMA, Wegovy EPAR, 2026). Ozempic is not approved for weight loss. Prescribing it for that purpose is off-label. The drug is also not covered as a routine benefit for weight use, because German law treats weight regulation as improving only "quality of life" — the financing rule that shapes the whole German market is set out below.

Wegovy reached the German market in mid-July 2023, with the higher dose costing €277.60 for a one-month pack (Pharmazeutische Zeitung, August 2026). Oral tablets were authorised by the EU in July 2026 and available from 1 September 2026, at about €172–233 per 30 days; a month later, pharmacies reported hesitant demand, with most patients staying on the weekly injection (Pharmazeutische Zeitung, August 2026; Apotheken Umschau, September 2026; dpa, October 2026).

## Does it work? Yes, for one to two years

The pivotal trial, STEP 1, randomised 1,961 adults without diabetes to semaglutide 2.4 mg or placebo for 68 weeks, both with a lifestyle programme. Mean weight change was −14.9% on the drug versus −2.4% on placebo. About 86% of treated participants lost at least 5% of body weight, 69% at least 10%, and 51% at least 15%, versus 32%, 12% and 5% on placebo (Wilding et al., NEJM, 2021). The two-year study, STEP 5, found the loss was maintained: −15.2% versus −2.6% at week 104 (Garvey et al., Nature Medicine, 2022).

Reduction is not guaranteed: about 14% of treated participants in STEP 1 lost less than 5%, and the EMA says treatment should be stopped in adolescents who have not lost 5% within the first 12 weeks (Wilding et al., NEJM, 2021; EMA, Wegovy EPAR, 2026).

The benefits are not only on the scales. In the SELECT trial of 17,604 overweight or obese patients with established cardiovascular disease but no diabetes, 2.4 mg semaglutide reduced the risk of heart attack, stroke or cardiovascular death by 20% over follow-up averaging about 40 months (hazard ratio 0.80) (Lincoff et al., NEJM, 2023). In patients with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease, the FLOW trial found a 24% lower risk of major kidney events (hazard ratio 0.76) (Perkovic et al., NEJM, 2024). These are large, well-run randomised trials; there is high confidence in their headline results.

## The catch: the effect is tied to the injection

The effect is not permanent. In the randomised withdrawal trial STEP 4, all participants lost weight during a 20-week run-in, then half continued semaglutide and half were silently switched to placebo for 48 weeks. The continuing group lost a further 7.9% of body weight; the placebo group regained 6.9% (Rubino et al., JAMA, 2021). In the year-long off-treatment extension of STEP 1, participants regained about two-thirds of the weight they had lost, and part of the blood-pressure and cholesterol improvement reverted too (Wilding et al., Diabetes Obes Metab, 2022). A 2026 pooled analysis of six trials estimated regain of about 1.0 kg per month after stopping, with half of the loss recovered within about 7.5 months, and return to baseline weight around 15 months (meta-analysis, Endocrinology Diabetes Metab, 2026).

Routine care is less tidy than the trials. A US academic obesity clinic found a median time on the drugs of 10.7 months, and patients who stayed at least a year lost a median 14.4% (Diabetes Obes Metab, 2025). A study of a German telemedicine weight-loss programme with 4,535 paying customers found a mean 12-month loss of 9.5%; only 12% of customers were still fully engaged at 12 months, and if drop-outs are counted as having lost nothing, the average falls to 2.4% (Diabetes Obes Metab, 2026). That cohort reflects paying telehealth customers rather than the general patient population, so it should not be read as the German average — but the pattern of early stopping shows up across cohorts.

The counter-argument deserves stating. Obesity is treated as a chronic disease, not a course of antibiotics: the point of a chronic drug is that it is continued. Nobody expects blood-pressure pills to keep working after stopping. The real-world problem is not biology but persistence and price — and in Germany the whole price falls on the patient.

## Safety: common nuisances, rare serious harms

Side effects are dominated by the gut: nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation and abdominal pain are the most common (EMA, Wegovy EPAR, 2026). In STEP 1, 4.5% of treated participants stopped because of gastrointestinal events versus 0.8% on placebo (Wilding et al., NEJM, 2021). Acute pancreatitis and gallbladder disease are listed warnings in the product information (DailyMed Wegovy label, 2025/2026). Both are rare but can be serious.

**Muscle.** Some of the weight lost is lean mass, not fat. A 2024 systematic review of six trials found lean-mass reductions ranging from nearly 0% to 40% of total weight lost (Expert Opin Pharmacother, 2024). In the SEMALEAN study of 106 patients, lean mass fell by about 3 kg in the first seven months and then levelled off, while handgrip strength improved by 4.5 kg at 12 months (Diabetes Obes Metab, 2026). The concern matters most for older adults at risk of sarcopenic obesity; resistance exercise and adequate protein are the standard mitigation (LEAN-PREP protocol, BMJ Open, 2026).

**Thyroid.** In rodents, semaglutide causes thyroid C-cell tumours, and the US label carries a boxed warning that human relevance is unknown. The drug is contraindicated in people with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2 syndrome (DailyMed Wegovy label, 2026). European post-authorisation studies on medullary thyroid cancer are listed by the EMA; no human signal has been established (EMA, Wegovy EPAR).

**Eye.** A 2024 observational study found an association with rare non-arteritic anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (NAION), a cause of sudden vision loss: hazard ratios of 4.3 in a diabetes cohort and 7.6 in an overweight/obesity cohort over 36 months, with small absolute numbers (Hathaway et al., JAMA Ophthalmology, 2024). A 2026 meta-analysis estimated roughly a tripled one-year risk in people with type 2 diabetes. These are observational associations, not proven causation, and cases remain rare.

**Mood.** After a review triggered by Icelandic case reports, the EMA's safety committee concluded in April 2024 that the available evidence does not support a causal link between GLP-1 receptor agonists and suicidal or self-injurious thoughts, and made no change to the product information (EMA PRAC, April 2024).

## The German dimension

Obesity is the medical setting in which these drugs are discussed: about one quarter of German adults have it, two-thirds of men and half of women are overweight, and so is about one child in six (Leopoldina, FOKUS Adipositas, January 2026). The societal cost of obesity and overweight is estimated at €113 billion a year, about 2.6% of German GDP (Leopoldina, 2026).

Germany's financing rule is the clearest barrier. Statutory health insurance may not pay for medicines whose primary aim is improving quality of life, which the law treats weight regulation as being — so Wegovy is classified as a lifestyle medicine and must be paid for privately (§ 34(1) SGB V; NDR, July 2026; Apotheken Umschau, September 2026). The same paragraph excludes smoking-cessation, potency and hair-growth remedies, so obesity drugs sit legally next to those rather than next to treatments for chronic disease (Pharmazeutische Zeitung, August 2026). Patients therefore pay about €172–278 per month indefinitely; the Spiegel's interview with the G-BA chairwoman puts the same price at "around €300 a month" for Wegovy or Mounjaro. Those on Ozempic for type 2 diabetes are covered by insurance; those using either drug purely for weight are not.

Whether that rule should change became an active political question in 2026. In July Sonja Optendrenk became the first woman to lead the Federal Joint Committee (G-BA), the body that decides what the statutory funds pay for. In a Spiegel interview she called Germany's debate on obesity backward, said it would be worth considering recognising weight-loss medicines as a statutory benefit under clear conditions, and pointed to the recent opening of the benefit catalogue to some smoking-cessation drugs as a precedent. She proposed that the Bundestag could commission the G-BA to decide which patient groups should receive the drugs, for how long and with what accompanying measures — and warned that once the weight comes straight back after stopping, the injection alone is no treatment concept, and that long-term studies are still missing (Spiegel, 2 August 2026). The insurance side is sceptical: Carola Reimann, chairwoman of the AOK fund group, warned that covering the drugs could cost the statutory funds billions (Pharmazeutische Zeitung/dpa, October 2026). At a Science Media Center briefing, health-law scholar Stefan Huster argued that BMI plus coexisting conditions could draw a defensible boundary, while nutrition researcher Hans Hauner put the bill at up to €50 billion a year if everyone who meets the licence criteria were treated at current prices (Pharmazeutische Zeitung, August 2026). Legal opinion is unanimous that the G-BA cannot change the rule itself: only the Bundestag can amend § 34 SGB V. The debate is also unfolding as a new federal health minister is installed: Optendrenk confirmed Carsten Linnemann (CDU), a newcomer to health policy, as the committee's commissioning authority (Spiegel, 2026).

The national academy of sciences also recommends changing the law so that proven obesity drugs can be reimbursed for people with severe obesity, arguing that the current rule denies patients an effective treatment (Leopoldina, FOKUS Adipositas, January 2026; tagesschau, January 2026). As of autumn 2026 no such change has been enacted.

The grey zone is online prescribing. Because a prescription is needed, part of the demand flows through online services, and the German pharmacy profession has told patients to have the drug prescribed only after a proper examination, not over the internet, and has warned that counterfeits circulate online (Apotheken Umschau, September 2026).

## Verdict and uncertainty

- **Works in the trials:** high confidence. Mean loss of roughly 15% at one and two years, with cardiovascular and kidney benefits in the relevant populations.
- **Works long-term in practice:** only while the drug is taken. The drugs do not "cure" obesity; they suppress appetite for as long as they are in the body. In Germany, where maintenance is privately funded, real-world persistence is low and average results are far below the trials.
- **Safe:** the common side effects are well characterised; serious harms are rare but real (pancreas, gallbladder, vision, muscle loss). Experience beyond five years is still building, so the distant tail of the risk curve is unknown. New longer-term data will revise this answer.

Overall: for people with serious obesity, especially with cardiovascular disease, the benefits on weight and complications are documented and large, and the drug works as long as it is taken. The main open questions are keeping patients on treatment and the unmeasured very-long-term safety. For people without a medical need, the risk–benefit balance is unfavourable, and in Germany they also bear the full cost.
